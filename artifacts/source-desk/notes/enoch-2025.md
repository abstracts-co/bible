# How old are these scrolls?

Popović et al. (2025), *PLOS ONE* 20(6): e0323185. [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0323185](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0323185)

**The question.** Almost none of the scrolls carry a date, so they're usually dated by handwriting, and that rests on a model of how the script developed that the authors say "is not reliably grounded" (p. 1). Can radiocarbon plus a handwriting model do better?

**What they did.** Radiocarbon-dated samples from 30 manuscripts, which gave 27 valid dates (p. 2). Then they trained a model, Enoch, on 24 of the dated manuscripts' handwriting, and asked it for dates for 135 undated ones (p. 1).

**What they report.** Enoch's predictions on the dated set were off by about 28–31 years on average (pp. 1, 5). Palaeographers *among the authors* judged 107 of the 135 new predictions (79%) realistic (p. 6). Many dates come out older than the usual handwriting estimates (p. 1). The figure shows this per manuscript: blue is radiocarbon, green Enoch, red the traditional handwriting date.

**Read carefully.** The blue bars are the "accepted" calibrated ranges, which is not the complete calibrated range (p. 2). A radiocarbon date is for the sample, so a copy's date isn't the date the text was first written. The 79% was judged by co-authors, not an outside panel.

**Still open for me:** when the predicted dates disagree with palaeography, who should we believe, and on what grounds? The paper calls this "a first step" (p. 8).
